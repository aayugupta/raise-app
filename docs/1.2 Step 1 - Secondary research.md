# Secondary research: why parenting content doesn't lead to conversations

Oct 22, 2026 · @Iuc

## Summary

**In one line:** Parents don't need more information. They need help to start the talk, to stay calm when their teen reacts, and a good moment to talk.

**Five key findings**

1. **Good intentions often stay intentions.** People act on their intentions only about half the time. Saving a post is an intention, not an action.
2. **The problem is starting, not knowing.** Parents in India and abroad say they don't know how to begin or what words to use.
3. **Comfort matters most.** Programmes that helped parents talk more did it by making them more comfortable, not just by giving them facts.
4. **India has the same gap, often wider.** 9 in 10 Indian parents worry about cyberbullying, but only 48% talk to their child about it (64% globally).
5. **Our survey shows it directly.** Of 14 parents who came across parenting content, only 3 then talked to their child.

**One honest note:** no study directly tracks whether *saved* parenting posts lead to real conversations. That link comes from our own survey, supported by the wider research below.

## 1. What parents say about parenting content

Parents use a lot of online content and find some of it useful. But they find it hard to know what to trust, and on difficult topics they still don't know how to start.

### Around the world

| Source | What it found |
| --- | --- |
| Mott Poll, US, 2023 (2,044 parents) | 80% of parents of young children use social media for parenting advice, but 40% find it hard to tell good advice from bad. |
| Information overload study, Sweden, 2023 (214 parents) | Less confident parents search more, but searching doesn't make them more confident. Too much information made them *less* confident a year later. |
| Gallup, US, 2024 (1,675 parent–child pairs) | About 1 in 5 parents often worry about a topic, such as social media or mental health, but find it hard to discuss. Yet 72–90% of children found even these hard talks helpful. |
| Wilson et al., US, 2010 (131 parents) | Parents agree these talks matter, but many haven't had them. The main reasons: "my child is too young" and "I don't know how to bring it up". |
| Jerman and Constantine, US, 2010 (907 parents) | About 70% of parents faced at least one difficulty, such as embarrassment or doubting their own knowledge. |
| Talk Soon. Talk Often., Australia (government guide) | Some parents worry so much about giving a perfect answer that they say nothing at all. |

### In India

| Source | What it found |
| --- | --- |
| McAfee, 2022 (10-country survey) | 9 in 10 Indian parents worry about cyberbullying, but only 48% talk to their child about it (64% globally). 45% of Indian children hide cyberbullying from parents (25% globally). |
| Ballal et al., coastal South India, 2022 (233 parents) | 202 parents said sexuality education is needed, but only 121 of them gave it themselves. Most of the rest said "children will learn on their own". |
| ParentCircle–IMRB, 2015 | 52% of parents would be extremely uncomfortable having "the talk". A third of children said they could never discuss it with parents. |
| Velocity MR, 8 metros (2,268 people) | 6 in 10 parents don't discuss sex education with their children. |
| Rashmi et al., Bengaluru, 2026 (100 mothers) | 52% of mothers had never discussed reproductive health with their daughters. Their main source of information was TV. |
| Likith, Bengaluru, 2024 (interviews with mothers) | Mothers said they didn't have the right words, and got little support from their husbands or family. |
| Population Council, 6 states, 2011 | Parents care, but social norms, lack of information and lack of communication skills hold them back. |
| UNICEF and Gallup, 2021 | Only 41% of young Indians think it's good to seek help for mental health problems (83% across 21 countries). |
| LocalCircles, 2025 (urban parents) | 2 in 3 parents say their child is addicted to social media, videos or games, and many don't know which apps their child uses. |

**What India adds:**

- Indian parents worry a lot but talk less than parents elsewhere.
- Finding the right words is a real problem; mothers in Bengaluru said so directly.
- The talk often falls on one parent, usually the mother.
- Parents' own information comes from TV, WhatsApp and family, not trusted guidance.

## 2. Six reasons saved posts don't turn into talks

"How sure we are" shows the strength of the evidence: **Strong** = large reviews of many studies; **Medium** = a few studies that agree; **Early** = only our own small survey.

| # | Reason | What the evidence says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Saving feels like doing, but it isn't.** | People act on their intentions only about half the time. Even when an intention gets much stronger, behaviour changes only a little. |
| 2 | **Posts explain the topic, not how to start.** | Parents' main problem is how to bring it up, and Indian mothers say they lack the words. Most posts give information, not a first sentence. |
| 3 | **A saved post has no reminder or moment.** | Busy people forget plans: in one experiment, 24% forgot an intended action when distracted, against 8% when not. Nothing links a saved post to a real moment, like a walk. |
| 4 | **Too much content lowers confidence.** | Less confident parents search more, but too much information makes them less confident over time. 40% of parents of young children can't tell good advice from bad. |
| 5 | **Content makes it feel like one big "Talk".** | Parents fear saying too much or getting it wrong. In India, social norms make it even harder. |
| 6 | **Content prepares the words, not the reaction.** | In our teen survey, 11 of 12 teens said lecturing or anger makes teens stop talking. Posts rarely help a parent handle that moment. |

## 3. What actually works

The approaches that changed what parents *do* have three things in common: a plan tied to a real moment, small repeated steps, and building comfort.

| What works | Evidence | Lesson for Raise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plan when and where you'll act** ("When we go for our Sunday walk, I'll say…") | A review of 94 studies: these "if-then" plans clearly beat good intentions alone. | Pair every opener with a chosen moment |
| **Small steps linked to daily routines** | 1,031 families got 3 short texts a week with one small tip tied to an existing routine. Parents did more of the activities at home. This was about early reading, so it's an example, not proof for our topic. | Small nudges, linked to routines, change behaviour |
| **Build parents' comfort** | A review of 28 parent programmes: parents talked more, and their comfort rose even more. | Help parents feel ready, not just informed |
| **Involve both parent and teen, over time** | A review of 31 trials (12,464 teens): communication improved, most for teens 14 and under, and when programmes lasted 10+ hours. | Long programmes work best, so a light app must prove itself |
| **Talk a little and often** | Government guidance in Australia. | Many short talks instead of one big one |

## 4. Does our own survey agree?

Yes. Our surveys (18 parents and 12 teens) match the published research on every point.

| Research says | Our survey found |
| --- | --- |
| Intentions often don't become action | 14 parents came across parenting content; only 3 then talked to their child |
| Starting is the hard part | 6 of 10 parents putting off a talk don't know how to begin or what words to use. 7 of 10 want help with "what to say first". None chose "the facts". |
| Comfort matters | Parents putting off a talk rated their confidence 2.8 out of 5; others rated it 3.9 |
| Everyday moments help | Teens prefer walks (9 of 12) and late night (7 of 12). Parents assumed meals and the car. |
| Parents compete with other sources | Only 1 of 12 teens named parents as a main source; most named friends (10) and Instagram (7) |
| Teens want calm parents who listen | 11 of 12 teens said lecturing or anger stops the talk; 11 of 12 said staying calm helps most |

## 5. What this means for Raise

| Because we found… | Raise will… |
| --- | --- |
| Saving a post rarely leads to a talk | Not be a content library. Each session prepares one specific talk. |
| Plans tied to a moment work best | Pair every opener with a chosen moment and a reminder at that time. |
| Parents struggle to start and fear the reaction | Give a ready first sentence, plus a "stay calm, listen, don't lecture" tip. |
| Too much information lowers confidence | Show one short, trusted prep card, not a feed. |
| Small, repeated steps change behaviour | Split each topic into short talks, with a nudge for the next one a week or two later. |
| Comfort is what changes | Measure parent confidence before and after, not just app use. |
| Indian parents lack the words and often talk alone | Write openers in natural Hinglish, and support fathers as well as mothers. |
| Long programmes work best | Be honest that a light app is unproven, test it in Step 6, and list it as a risk in Step 8. |
