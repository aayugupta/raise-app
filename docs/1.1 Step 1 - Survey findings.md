**Teens.** 12 teens answered the teen survey on 22 October, all with a parent's permission: 8 aged 16–18 and 4 aged 14–15. **10 are girls and only 2 are boys,** so the teen findings mostly reflect girls. Teens and parents were not matched by family, so the comparison is between the two groups, not within households.

## What parents told us

10 of 18 parents have a talk they are putting off: 6 for a few weeks, 3 for a few months, and 1 who started once but didn't get far.

**1. The blocker is the first sentence, not the facts.** Of the 10 parents postponing a talk, 6 picked "I'm not sure how to begin" or "I can't find words that feel right". Only 2 said they weren't sure of the facts. Asked what would make it easiest, 7 of 10 chose "knowing what to say first", and none chose "having the facts clear". This directly backs the opener as Raise's core feature.

**2. Content doesn't turn into conversations.** Of the 14 parents who had come across a parenting article, reel or video, only 3 then talked to their child. 6 saved or shared it, and 5 thought about it and forgot. This is the brief's "why not more content" argument, in your own numbers.

**3. Adult content online is the most-postponed topic, and some topics aren't on parents' radar at all.** Adult content was named by 5 of the 10 postponing parents, more than any other topic, and only 7 of 18 have already talked about it. Consent and personal safety (6 of 18) and gender, sexuality and LGBTQ (3 of 18) are the least-covered topics, yet no parent listed them as a talk they're planning. Those topics are not postponed; they are not on the list.

**A pattern to test in interviews:** all 3 fathers of daughters are postponing a talk, against 1 of 5 fathers of sons. The numbers are far too small to claim, but it fits Indian studies showing fathers talk about these topics far less than mothers, so ask about it directly.

Confidence tracks postponement. Parents with a pending talk averaged 2.8 out of 5 on confidence, and 5 of the 10 scored 2. Parents with nothing pending averaged 3.9.

## What teens told us: the conversation gap

Teens and parents want different talks. The biggest gap is relationships: **10 of 12 teens wish their parents talked about relationships and dating more**, but it isn't the talk most parents are planning. Parents are most stuck on 18+ content online, which only 2 of 12 teens asked for.

&#91;embedded content: Parent survey Q6 (10 parents with a pending talk) and teen survey Q5 (12 teens), 19–22 Oct 2026 · teen topic labels matched to the parent list\]

**Teens want the relationships talk, if it's done without judgement.** Asked what they want from parents about crushes, dating and relationships, 10 of 12 teens chose "talk about it openly, without judging". Only 2 said parents shouldn't talk about it at all.

**How parents react matters as much as how they start.** 11 of 12 teens said a lecture or a parent getting angry makes teens stop talking, and 11 of 12 said talks go much better when parents stay calm (8 also chose "listen more than they talk"). Parents worry about the first sentence; teens worry about the reaction that follows. Raise needs to help with both.

**Parents are not where teens learn.** Only 1 of 12 teens named parents as a main source for these topics. Friends (10 of 12), Instagram (7) and web series or movies (5) fill the gap.

**Fathers are rarely the easy parent to talk to.** No teen chose "father" as easier to talk to about personal topics. 4 chose mother, 5 said it depends on the topic, and 3 chose another family member. With only 2 boys in the sample, this mostly reflects girls, but it matches the parent finding that fathers of daughters are the ones putting talks off.

**Teens welcome the talks, depending on how they're done.** Only 1 of 12 said teens would rather not talk; 5 said it depends on how the parent does it. On Raise itself, 8 of 12 said teens would be fine with, or wouldn't mind, a parent using an app to prepare, 4 found it "a little strange", and none chose "like the parent is trying to control them".

Fear-type barriers (awkwardness, the child's reaction, sparking curiosity) were picked by 5 of the 10, so the prep card also needs a calm "if they go quiet" line, not just the opener.

## Design inputs

Both surveys together settle the main build decisions for v1. Where parents and teens disagree, Raise follows the teens, because the talk only works if the teen stays in it.

| Decision | Parents (of 18) | Teens (of 12) | What it means for Raise |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Moment picker | Meals 11, cooking or chores 11, walks 9, car 8, late night 7, text 5 | Walks 9, late night 7, cooking or chores 6, meals 3, car 1, text 1 | Lead with walks and chores, then late night. Meals and the car, parents' favourites, work least for teens. |
| Prep card tone | The block is how to begin | Calm and listening matter most (see the gap section above) | Every prep card needs a "stay calm, listen more, don't lecture" line next to the opener. |
| Who to support | All 3 fathers of daughters are putting a talk off | No teen chose father as easier to talk to | Treat fathers as a key segment, with openers that don't rely on the mother. |
| Language | Mix of Hindi and English 14, Hindi 4, English only 0 | Not asked | Hinglish by default, with a Hindi option. |
| Reminder channel | WhatsApp 10, app notification 5, SMS 1, no reminders 2 | Not asked | Nudge on WhatsApp; for the MVP, send it by hand. |
| Privacy | Not asked | 8 fine or wouldn't mind a parent using an app, 4 "a little strange", none "controlling" | Teens accept it; keep Raise discreet and never show the teen anything from it. |
| Teen-world briefs | 5 would like to know more about their child's world | Only 1 named parents as a main source | Keep "what teens face today" as a short part of the prep card. |

**Step 6 baseline:** average confidence about starting a difficult talk is 3.3 out of 5 across all 18 parents, and 2.8 among the 10 postponing one. Ask the same question after parents use Raise and compare.
