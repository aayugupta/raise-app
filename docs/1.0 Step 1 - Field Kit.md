## 10-day plan

Interviews finish by Day 4, the app goes live on Day 6, and parents use it over the weekend so there are real completed conversations to report by Day 10. The one move that makes this work: **every parent you interview is asked to be a first user.** Your 10 interviewees become your first 10 users, and you only need 10 more.

The brief defines completion within 14 days, but you will have only 3–4 days of usage. Report it honestly as an early rate ("X of Y topics completed in the first 4 days") and project the 14-day number in Step 6. Assessors respect that more than an inflated figure.

## Outreach messages

Lead with "20 minutes, no personal details, for a course project." Parents say yes to a small, safe, student ask. Avoid the words "sex education" in the first message, because they shut doors before you explain. Name the broad area instead (money, growing up, online life).

**Where to send it, in order of yield:** your own relatives and family friends with teens → colleagues at work who are parents → your society / RWA WhatsApp group → school parent WhatsApp groups (ask one parent to forward it) → a local tuition centre for classes 9–12 (ask the owner to share it with parents).

**WhatsApp — direct (English)**

> Hi \[Name\], I'm doing a product-management course project on how parents talk to teenagers about things school doesn't cover — money, growing up, what they see online. Could I take 20 minutes of your time on a call this week? No personal details needed, and nothing is shared with your name. Your child is in class 9–12, right? Would \[day\] or \[day\] work?

**In person (school gate / society / tuition centre), 15 seconds**

> "I'm building a small app from what parents tell me. Could I send it to you on Saturday to try once?"

## Parent survey (14 questions, \~4 min)

The survey follows one thread: the talk a parent keeps putting off, what holds them back, and what would help them start. All 14 questions are required. It is anonymous: the three "about you" questions let you compare groups (mothers vs fathers, younger vs older teens) without identifying anyone.

**Form intro:**

| Instead of | We say | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "haven't yet" (Q5) | "the right moment hasn't come yet" | Puts the delay on circumstance, not on the parent |
| "what's stopping you" (Q7) | "what makes it hard to start" | Describes difficulty, not failure |
| "they'll get upset" | "might feel upset" / "bura maan sakte hain" | Softer, and doesn't predict the child's reaction |
| "bachcha chup ho jayega" | "bachche chup ho sakte hain" | Plural-respectful Hindi avoids assuming the child is a boy |
| "your partner should do it" | "someone else (school, doctor, family)" | Doesn't assume two parents in the home |
| "what's normal for kids" | "what kids this age face today" | Avoids judging any child as abnormal |
| "how well do you know your child's world" (Q10) | "how up to date do you feel" | A feeling, not a test; the lowest option is "I'd like to know more" |
| "I don't watch such content" (Q9) | "I haven't come across such content" | No suggestion that the parent should have |
| "rarely opens up" (Q11) | "such moments are hard to find" | Doesn't label the child or the relationship |
| "Mother / Father" only | "parents and guardians", with "Other guardian" in Q2 | Includes grandparents, single parents and other caregivers |
| "Sex and relationships" | "Relationships and physical intimacy" | Clear but respectful, so parents don't close the form |

## Qualitative research plan

The survey told you *what* parents feel; the interviews explain *why*, and give you the quotes and stories your deck needs. Target: **8–10 parent interviews and 5–8 teen interviews by Saturday 24 Oct**, about 20 minutes each, by phone or video.

**Who to interview**

| Who | How many | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parents with a talk they're putting off | 6–7 | They are Raise's user; hear exactly where they get stuck |
| Fathers, including 2+ fathers of daughters | At least 3 | Tests the survey pattern (all 3 fathers of daughters were postponing) |

**Where to find them:** survey respondents who messaged you on WhatsApp first (they're already interested), then your personal network. At the end of every parent interview, ask whether their teen would talk to you for 15 minutes, and whether they know another parent you could call.

**Survey-led probes.** Weave these six questions into your parent interviews. Each one digs into a survey finding.

| Survey finding | Ask in the interview |
| --- | --- |
| 7 of 10 want "what to say first" | "If you had to start tonight, what's the very first sentence you'd say?" Then: "What makes that sentence hard to say?" |
| 6 of 10 can't find how to begin or the words | "Which words feel uncomfortable? Is it easier in Hindi or in English?" |
| 11 of 14 saved or forgot parenting content | "Tell me about the last thing you saved. What stopped you from using it?" |
| Adult content online is the most postponed | "What worries you about what your child might see online? What would you want them to know?" |
| Consent and LGBTQ are rarely covered, and nobody is planning to raise them | "Some topics, like consent, came up very little. Why do you think that is?" (Ask neutrally, never as a judgement.) |
| Fathers of daughters are all postponing | For fathers: "Is there anything you feel your daughter's mother should cover instead of you? Why?" |

**How to run each interview**

1. Confirm 20 minutes, and say again that no names are recorded and they can skip any question.
2. Take written notes. Record audio only if they agree out loud, and delete it once your notes are done.
3. Let them speak in Hindi, English or a mix, and note quotes in their own words.
4. Ask what they *did*, not what they *would* do; don't pitch Raise until the very end.
5. End by asking: "Can I send you the app on Saturday to try?" and "Would your teen talk to me?"
6. Within an hour, fill in one row of the parent log in the Notes template, then paste your raw notes to Claude for synthesis.

## Parent interview guide (20–25 min)

Ask about what they *did*, never what they *would* do. "Would you use an app for this?" gets a polite yes and tells you nothing. "Tell me about the last time you tried" gets the truth. Don't pitch your idea and don't fill silences. The useful line usually comes after a pause.

**Opening (2 min)**

> "Thank you. This is for a course project. There are no right answers, and I won't use your name. I'm not asking about anything private in your family; I'm interested in what makes certain conversations hard to start. You can skip any question. OK if I take notes?"

Note down: child's class (9/10/11/12), parent's role (mother/father/other), city type. Do not record the child's name.

**1. The postponed conversation (8 min) — the heart of the interview**

1. "Is there a conversation you've been meaning to have with your child, but haven't yet?" *(Koi aisi baat hai jo aap apne bachche se karna chahte the, lekin abhi tak nahi ki?)*
2. "What's the topic, broadly?" (Let them name it. Don't offer a list yet.)
3. "When did you first think, 'I should talk about this'? What triggered it?"
4. "What has stopped you so far?" Probe: not knowing what to say / timing / fear of their reaction / fear of saying too much / not my job (other parent's).
5. "Have you ever started it? What happened?" If yes: "What was the first sentence you said? How did they react? What did you do next?"
6. "If you had 10 minutes before that conversation, what would you need in your hand?"

**2. Their own history (3 min)**

7. "Did your parents ever talk to you about things like this?" *(Aapke mummy-papa ne kabhi aise topics pe baat ki thi?)*
8. "Where did you learn about it yourself?"

**3. The topic map (4 min)**

Only now, show or read the starting list: money and banking, periods and puberty, consent and personal safety, what they see online (including porn), how to behave with others.

9. "Which of these have you already covered? Which feel hardest? Is anything missing?"
10. "Which one do you think your child most needs right now? Which does your child *think* they need?"

**4. What's changed (3 min)**

11. "What's in your child's world now that wasn't there when you were 15?"
12. "Is there something you've heard about (an app, a trend) that worries you? Where did you hear it?" Note whether it's from a news story / WhatsApp forward (possible panic) or something they saw directly.

**5. Content they've tried (3 min)**

13. "Have you ever read, watched or saved something about how to talk to teenagers? What was it?"
14. "What did you do after saving it?" The answer is usually "nothing". Ask why.

**Close (2 min)**

15. "Is there anything I should have asked?"
16. Ask for the teen interview (only if they seem comfortable) and to try the app on Saturday.

**What to listen for:** the exact words they use for a topic (these become your app's vocabulary), the first sentence they did or didn't say, and the moment their energy changed.

## Teen interview guide (15 min)

Ask every question about "people your age", not "you". The third-person framing keeps it about topics and sources, as the brief requires, and teens answer more honestly when they're not talking about themselves. If a teen starts sharing something personal, gently steer back: "Thanks — let's keep it general, I'm just mapping topics." If they mention anything that suggests they're unsafe, stop the interview and tell them who they can talk to (a trusted adult, the school counsellor, or Childline 1098).

**Opening (1 min)**

> "I'm building something for parents, not for teens, and you're the expert on what parents get wrong. Nothing about your own life. Just what people your age think. You can skip anything or stop. OK?"

**1. What's missing (4 min)**

1. "What do people your age wish someone had explained to them properly?"
2. "Which of those does school cover? How well?"
3. Read the starting list (money, growing up and bodies, consent and safety, online life, how to treat people). "Which matter most to people your age? Which do adults think matter, but don't really?"

**2. Where people learn things (3 min)**

4. "When someone your age has a question they can't ask at home, where do they go?" (Friends / YouTube / Instagram / Reddit / AI chatbots / older siblings)
5. "How much do people trust those sources?"

**3. Talking to parents (5 min) — the most valuable part**

6. "When a parent tries to bring up one of these topics, what makes a teen shut it down in five seconds?"
7. "What could a parent say or do that would make it *not* awkward?"
8. "Is there a better time or place for these talks? (car, walk, text, dinner)"
9. "Would people your age rather a parent bring it up, or wait to be asked?"

**4. What parents don't get (2 min)**

10. "What's normal for people your age now that parents are behind on?"
11. "What do parents worry about that's actually not a big deal?" (This separates real shifts from adult moral panic.)

**Close**

> "Anything you'd tell a parent who's nervous to start? Thank you, this really helps."

**What to listen for:** the shut-down triggers (Q6) and the panic gap (Q11). Those two answers will shape your product more than anything else.
