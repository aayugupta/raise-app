# 3 Sharp Insights

## Insight 1: Parents don't lack information. They lack the first line.

> Parents already know *what* to talk about. They get stuck on *how to start*. More articles won't help. A ready first sentence will.

**Evidence**

| Signal | % |
| --- | --- |
| Delaying parents who don't know how to begin or can't find the words | **60%** |
| Delaying parents who want "what to say first" as help | **70%** |
| Delaying parents who want more facts | **0%** |
| Parents who saw useful content but never used it (saved or forgot it) | **79%** |
| Parents who saw content and then actually talked | 21% |

- Indian mothers say the same thing in research: they lack the words, not the will.
- Research on behaviour change finds that good intentions often fail to turn into action. A specific "when and how" plan ("when X happens, I will say Y") has a medium-to-large effect on actually following through.

**What it changes in Raise**

- **Build:** a ready **first line** for every topic, in Hinglish, Hindi or English, short enough to say out loud.
- **Build:** a nudge at the right moment ("On tonight's walk, try: ..."), not a reading list.
- **Don't build:** an article and video library as the core. Content is support, not the product.

## Insight 2: Teens shut down at the reaction, not the topic.

> Teens aren't refusing the topics. They're avoiding the lecture, the anger and the punishment that follow. Whether a talk works depends on how the parent reacts in the first minute.

**Evidence**

| Signal | % |
| --- | --- |
| Teens who say a lecture or anger stops the talk | **92%** |
| Teens who say a calm parent makes it better | **92%** |
| Teens who say listening makes it better | 67% |
| Teens who call a parent talk app "controlling" | **0%** |
| Teen reviews of parental control apps that give one star | **76%** |
| Indian children who faced cyberbullying and hid it from parents | 45% |

- Doctors at India's tech-addiction clinic say teens hide problems because they fear losing the phone.
- Teens reject control apps as "too strict" and "used instead of talking to me". They don't reject an app that helps parents talk.
- Both sides fear the other's reaction: 20% of delaying parents worry about how their teen will react.

**What it changes in Raise**

- **Build:** coaching for the **parent's reaction**: what to say, what *not* to say, and a "you won't be in trouble" safety promise.
- **Build:** a **repair script** for after a talk goes wrong ("I reacted badly yesterday. Can we try again?").
- **Measure success** by whether the teen kept talking or came back later, not by how many topics were ticked.
- **Don't build:** tracking, blocking or monitoring of any kind.

## Insight 3: Same topics, different sides. Lead with the teen's side.

> Parents start from the risk: adult content, phones, safety. Teens want to talk about relationships, feelings and friends. The topics parents have already "covered" are the ones teens want no more of, and teens learn about everything else from friends and Instagram.

**Evidence**

| Signal | Parents | Teens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Want more talk on relationships |  | **83%** |
| Want more talk on feelings, or on friends |  | **58%** each |
| Delayed talk is about adult content | **50%** | Only 17% want more on it |
| Have covered phone, online safety, body image | 56% to 67% | **0%** want more |
| Learn mainly from parents |  | **8%** (friends 83%, Instagram 58%) |
| Best moment to talk | Meals 61%, car 44% | Walks **75%**, late night 58%, car 8% |

- In Indian studies, 17% to 87% of adolescents have never discussed sexual topics with a parent. So the relationships talk is the biggest missing piece.
- More teens say they learned a lot about sex from a trusted adult (47%) than from porn (27%). Parents can still be the main source if they start the relationships talk.

**What it changes in Raise**

- **Build:** topic cards that open from the **teen's question** ("How do you know someone likes you back?") and bring safety in along the way.
- **Build:** nudges timed to teen-friendly moments (walks, late evenings), not the dinner table.
- **Build:** a short "what's in your teen's world" explainer (Instagram, web series, friends), so parents know where the talk is already happening.
- **Don't build:** separate safety lectures as the main path.

## What changes in the build

| Before research we might have built | After these insights we build | Insight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A library of articles and videos | A ready **first line** for each topic, in the parent's language | 1 |
| Reminders to "read more" | **Moment nudges**: "On tonight's walk, try..." | 1, 3 |
| Topics framed around risks | Topics that open from the **teen's question**, with safety inside | 3 |
| Facts for the parent to deliver | Coaching for the **parent's reaction**, plus a safety promise | 2 |
| No help once a talk goes badly | A **repair script** to try again | 2 |
| Monitoring and control features | **None.** A talk tool, not a spy tool | 2 |
| Success = topics covered | Success = **the teen kept talking or came back** | 2 |

## What to check in interviews

*These insights rest on small surveys (18 parents, 12 teens, mostly girls aged 16 to 18). The interviews should confirm or break them.*

| Insight | Confirmed if interviews show... | Broken if... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. First line | Parents describe freezing at the start, or rehearsing and never saying it | Parents mainly ask for facts or expert answers |
| 2. Reaction | Teens recall a talk that ended because a parent got angry or lectured | Teens say the topic itself is what they avoid |
| 3. Teen's side | Teens raise relationships and feelings first, and parents raise risks first | Both sides name the same priorities |

**Watch also:** fathers. In our survey, all 3 fathers of daughters are putting off a talk, and no teen picked their father as easier to talk to. That is too few to call an insight yet, but it could become a fourth one.
